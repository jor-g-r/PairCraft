Maldonado sits on Uruguay's southeastern tip, where the Atlantic and the Río de la Plata collide. The soils shift between decomposed granite and sandy loam — lean, well-drained, hostile in the best way. This is not Canelones. The region is younger in ambition, with serious producers arriving in the last two decades drawn by cooler temperatures and the promise of structure that the interior can't deliver.

The stylistic signature is tension: wines here carry more acid and less residual warmth than Uruguayan norms. Tannat loses some of its bruising density and gains length. Albariño, planted experimentally, shows genuine salinity. Watch Garzón — the sub-zone and the producer — as the benchmark. Bodega Garzón's single-vineyard work on granite hillsides is the clearest argument that Maldonado is Uruguay's most serious fine-wine address.
